# Cost-to-Claim Ratio Variance Analysis

In the context of the Hiscox Insurance example, **variance** refers to the statistical measure of how far each claim's cost deviates from the average cost-to-claim ratio.

Here's a breakdown of how variance works in this scenario:

## Cost-to-Claim Ratio

The cost-to-claim ratio is a key performance indicator that compares the total cost of a claim (payouts, legal fees, administrative costs) to the premium received for that policy. A high ratio indicates that the insurer is paying out a significant amount relative to the premium earned, which could mean the policy is underpriced.

---

## Uncovering Variances

**Variances** in this context would highlight claims that are significantly more or less expensive than the average.

- **Positive Variance**: A claim with a significantly **higher** cost-to-claim ratio than the average. These are the claims that are "leaky" and losing the company money.
- **Negative Variance**: A claim with a significantly **lower** cost-to-claim ratio than the average. These claims might indicate a policy is priced too high for the risk, but the focus is usually on the high-cost variances.

By analyzing the variance, the company could see which specific policies, types of claims, or even geographical areas had costs that were unexpectedly high. For example, if the average cost-to-claim ratio for a certain type of policy was 0.6 (meaning 60 cents in costs for every dollar of premium), but a specific group of policies had a ratio of 0.9, that would be a significant variance.

---

## Enabling Premium Adjustment

Discovering these variances allowed Hiscox to make data-driven decisions. The **$750K premium adjustment** was likely achieved by:

- **Increasing Premiums**: Adjusting the pricing of policies that were found to be consistently underpriced. For example, if a specific risk category had a high positive variance, they would raise the premium for new policies in that category to better reflect the true risk.
- **Altering Underwriting**: Modifying the criteria for who they insure. If certain customer profiles consistently had high-variance claims, they might tighten their underwriting standards for those groups.
- **Negotiating Better Terms**: In some cases, the variance might point to a need for better legal counsel or claims management to reduce the costs associated with claims.
